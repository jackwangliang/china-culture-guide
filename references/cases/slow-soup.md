## Part 2 — Model answer (English, as a foreign user would receive it)

*Zhǔ tāng* (煮汤) means simply "to make a soup by boiling"; *bāo tāng* (煲汤) is a Cantonese term meaning both a **steep-sided earthenware pot** and "to simmer a soup in it over a low flame for hours". So the difference is not two kinds of soup — it is two regimes of **time, vessel and heat**.

**[A note on the premise, checked first]** It is very natural to frame this as "our quick soup vs their slow soup": travel writing and restaurant menus do it constantly. Both halves need adjusting. Most everyday Chinese soups are quick — 滚汤 (gǔn tāng, "rolled soup") and egg-drop or vegetable soups take minutes. And long-simmered stock is not foreign to Western kitchens: French *fond* and bone broth are routinely cooked for many hours. What is actually being compared is a **specific Cantonese practice**, 老火靓汤 (lǎo huǒ liàng tāng, "old-fire good soup"), with **industrial-era convenience soup**. The real variable is when a household could afford hours of fuel and a whole pot of meat.

**[L5 Language & symbols]** These verbs form a technical grammar, not synonyms: 煮 (zhǔ) is generic boiling; 煲 (bāo) is low, long, covered simmering in a clay pot; 炖 (dùn) is gentler still, often a sealed vessel or water bath, giving a clear broth; 熬 (áo) emphasises reduction and concentration. The industry saying 三煲四炖 ("three hours to 煲, four to 炖") encodes time in the verb. Two older shifts are easy to miss: 汤 originally meant **hot water** (*Shuowen*: 汤, 热水也), and only from the Tang onward did it mean a dish with broth — earlier, 羹 (gēng) was the word for a thick soup or stew. And 煲 is a **regional character**, absent from standard historical dictionaries; a Guangzhou newspaper column traces a Tang-era homophone (褒牛头 in *Beihu Lu*) and notes it in Qing-era Cantonese dictionaries compiled by missionaries. [B/C — attribute, do not state as settled philology.]

**[L6 Time & variation, with L2 Economy]** Boiling meat and bones in clay is genuinely old: a bronze 鼎 from the Western Han Nanyue royal tomb (Guangzhou) has soot on the base and pig, chicken and fish remains inside. [A — excavated object.] But the modern package — **medicinal ingredients, generous meat, three hours, drinking the liquid and discarding the solids** — is not. Research by food historian 林卫辉, reviewed in provincial media and reposted by the Guangzhou municipal cyberspace office, finds no 老火靓汤 in the late-Qing/early-Republic Cantonese recipe collection *Meiwei Qiuzhen* (182 dishes), nor in Republican-era Cantonese restaurants in Guangzhou, Hong Kong or Shanghai; the historian 周松芳 reached the same conclusion. The earliest clear written record of the modern practice is an anthropologist's 1974–75 dietary survey of a family in Hong Kong's Castle Peak Bay (E. N. Anderson and wife, 152 main meals), where long simmering with tonic herbs appears twice, both when an adult was unwell. It entered Pearl River Delta restaurants in the 1980s and peaked in the 1990s with TV soup columns and 佘自强's *Today's Soup*. [B — official site and provincial book review, corroborating one another; the competing "2,700 years / Tang soup banquet" claim is a C-tier encyclopaedia entry and should be named as the popular version, not adopted.]

Why so late? Because of **cost**, which is what the economy lens asks about. A 1740 memorial from the governor-general of Guangdong complains that firewood was so expensive ordinary people struggled to cook; household account books from 1832–49 and later estimates suggest a Guangzhou family might eat meat on roughly twenty days a year. Three hours of fuel plus a pot of meat whose solids are thrown away was not an option. The everyday soup of that era was the quick 滚汤 — deliberately fast *to save fuel*. The practice became possible when Hong Kong households grew affluent in the 1950s–70s, then spread back to the mainland. [B — official-site essay citing the memorial and account books; figures are historical estimates, not precise statistics.] Note the five stages: old vessel → old word → old boiled broths → **modern standardisation** → recent popularisation → today's 15-minute soup kits and one-person electric stew pots. It is still changing.

**[L1 Material & technology, with L4]** The clay pot is physics before it is symbolism. Earthenware conducts heat far more slowly than iron or stainless steel and stores more of it, so the pot heats evenly, avoids scorching, holds a bare simmer rather than a rolling boil, and keeps cooking after the flame is off. Adding sand or shell temper to the clay (夹砂陶, attested in Neolithic cooking vessels) deflects cracks and improves resistance to thermal shock — which is also why a clay pot still cracks if you rush it on a modern gas flame. The ceramic body is chemically fairly inert, so it does not react with acidic or herbal ingredients. [B — a materials-science popular article; the numerical conductivity values there are indicative, not a measurement made here.]. **Separate that from the story attached to it**: "clay makes the soup more nourishing" is a later cultural reading, and popular claims about far-infrared effects or micropores "creating umami" are not supported at normal stewing temperatures. Modern electric stew pots and pressure cookers reproduce most of the physical effect; Denis Papin demonstrated a pressure digester to the Royal Society in 1679. [B — food-history reference.]

The classical vocabulary follows the technique rather than causing it. *Lüshi Chunqiu · Benwei* has 伊尹 saying "凡味之本，水最为始。五味三材，九沸九变，火为之纪" — water first, repeated boilings, fire as the regulator — a craftsman's description of long wet cooking, used in the text as a metaphor for governing. Qing 袁枚's *Suiyuan Shidan* splits 火候 into 武火 for frying and 文火 for simmering: "火猛则物枯矣". 煲汤 is the slow end of a single technical idea, 火候, not the offspring of a cosmological doctrine. [A/B — institutional essays quoting the classics; quotations should be checked against original texts for scholarly use.]

**[L3 Bridge]** If you grew up with stock cubes, canned soup or a pressure cooker, "minutes" is a 20th-century achievement too — just one your kitchen acquired earlier and more completely. In many Cantonese homes the long pot is less about nutrition than about care: the 1974 record shows herbs appearing when someone in the family was ill, and 回家饮汤 ("come home and drink soup") was a family phrase. [Health note: Chinese health-communication sources widely state that only a small fraction of meat protein dissolves into the broth while purines rise with cooking time; this is context, not medical advice — readers with health concerns should check official sources or a clinician.]

**[Closers]** Search `老火靓汤 起源 考证`, `滚汤 老火汤 区别`, `砂锅 材质 原理`, `吕氏春秋 本味 火为之纪` on WeChat 搜一搜 or a video platform. Today: find one short video of 滚汤 and one of 老火汤 and note the difference in heat, not just in time.

## Part 4 — What this case teaches

1. **A comparison is often a genesis question in disguise.** When a "why is X different" question arrives, promote it; a dimension table is a presentation format, not an explanation.
2. **Search must be driven by the claims in the answer, not by the archetype label.** This question was tagged "Comparison — Optional", yet every interesting sentence needed verification. That defect is fixed in Step 3, and the Step 0a gate now makes live search a prerequisite for the whole cultural domain.
3. **The most valuable evidence can be a conflict between sources**, not a single strong one. Showing "official and scholarly sources say ~60 years, a popular encyclopaedia says 2,700" teaches the user more than picking the confident-sounding number. Never average them.
4. **Cost questions do the explanatory work.** L2's "what did it cost in fuel, meat and time?" is what produced the real answer; no cultural preference needed to be invented.
5. **The anti-regression test passed.** Not one entry was added to `anchors.md`: 火候, 水火相济, 鼎/镬/釜, 分餐/合食 and the eight error patterns were already there and were reused. The new knowledge went into a lens operator and this case — exactly where it belongs.
6. **Premise checks now have more than one shape.** This case added false symmetry and time-depth error (plus misplaced place/class) to the original wrong-cause pattern; all four are listed in Step 1.
