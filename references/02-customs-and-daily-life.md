## The question

> "Why does everyone insist on paying the whole bill? And why do people give red envelopes?"

## Replaying the framework

**Step 1, classify.** **Meaning** (what does the behaviour signal?) + **Society** (what does it do to the relationship?) + **Time** (is this ancient or recent?). Not Procedure — the user is not asking how to pay.

**Step 2, lenses.** Society & family (reciprocity, host/guest), Ideas & cosmology (auspicious colour and homophones), Language & symbols (字面 vs 寓意), Economy (who can afford to treat), Time & variation (regional, generational, digital forms).

**Step 3, evidence.** The Step 0a search gate applies to the **whole cultural domain**, including a purely explanatory question: 人情 (reciprocity), 面子 (face), 礼 (role-based propriety) and 谐音 (homophones) all have common misreadings that only a source check catches. `anchors.md` concepts generate the queries and organise the retrieved evidence; they are **not an offline answer source**. If search is unavailable, stop and use the NO-SEARCH CONTRACT instead of writing an explanation. A "when did 红包 start?" follow-up adds mandatory date verification.

**Step 4, generate.** Model shape:

> Short answer: paying is a warmth signal inside a long-term reciprocity pattern, not a test or a trick; 红包 (hóngbāo, red envelope) is a way of transferring luck and care in a form that avoids the awkwardness of handing over cash.
>
> **[Society]** 人情 / rénqíng is a long-running exchange of small obligations. The host treating today and the guest treating next time keeps the relationship open. Refusing once or twice is part of the same polite exchange, not a negotiation. Among younger urban friends, splitting (AA 制 / AA zhì) is common — ask casually.
>
> **[Ideas and language]** Red is auspicious; the gift is about the envelope and the occasion, not the sum. Folk associations with numbers and homophones (八 / bā ~ 发 / fā, prosperity; 四 / sì ~ 死 / sǐ) are widely known but not rules everyone follows. Upside-down 福 works because 倒 (dào, upside down) sounds like 到 (dào, arrive).
>
> **[Time and variation]** Amounts, who gives to whom, and whether a family observes a taboo vary strongly by region and generation. Digital red envelopes in WeChat are a recent form of an older pattern. Do not call any of this "ancient Chinese law".
>
> **[Bridge]** Compare it to a host insisting on cooking for a guest rather than to a transaction.
>
> **[Uncertainty]** No single rule covers all families; if giving a gift or attending a wedding, ask a local friend or check a current, specific guide.
>
> **[Closers]** Search `红包 来历`, `人情 面子 科普`. Today: learn 谢谢，下次我请客 / xièxie, xià cì wǒ qǐngkè ("thanks, next time it's my treat") and use it from `09-phrasebook.md`.

## What this case demonstrates

- The same five lenses answer hundreds of customs questions — hot water, slippers, "have you eaten?", gift taboos, seating, toasts — **without an entry for each one**.
- Explaining the *why* (reciprocity, face, homophones, seasonal timing) is more portable than listing rules.
- Regional and generational variation is part of the answer, not a footnote.

## Recurring traps for customs questions

See the eight error patterns in `anchors.md`: over-generalisation, internet elaboration as classical doctrine, translation equivalence (面子 ≠ "vanity"), and backwards causality (a folk explanation presented as the historical origin). Never write "Chinese people always…". For ready-made bilingual lines, use `09-phrasebook.md` rather than growing this file.
